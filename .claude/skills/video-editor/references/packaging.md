# Packaging and ideas (advice mode)

Read this when the user asks about titles, thumbnails, video ideas, or why
a video isn't getting clicks. This is advice, not editing, but a strong
edit can't save a video nobody clicks. Leo's view is that these tactics
are also **the criteria for a good video idea:** if you can't apply them
to the title and thumbnail, nobody will watch the video.

## How thumbnails are actually seen

- **About 1 second, tiny, among competitors.** On mobile, a video starts
  autoplaying after about 1 second without scrolling. The thumbnail must be
  scannable in that time.
  → `scripts/thumb_check.py thumb.png --compare a.png b.png` renders it at
  mobile feed, desktop, and sidebar sizes plus grayscale, and reports
  brightness contrast, colorfulness, and a clutter proxy. Compare with
  thumbnails the user's audience actually clicks.
- **The thumbnail is seen before the title.** It should be intriguing and
  make sense on its own.
- **Getting noticed first is the most pivotal advantage.** Recognition,
  contrast, and alarm are what win the first glance.

## The reason to watch

- **Every viral video has at least one captivating reason to watch, and
  it's immediately apparent.** If it takes more than a second to think of,
  or needs rationalizing, it's too weak.
- **Common reasons:** a simple, universally intriguing question; dropping
  the viewer into the middle of a story; the promise of an exciting
  journey; something interesting to learn.
- **The title and thumbnail build on each other and never repeat.**
  Repetition wastes the one second you have. Use both to stack as many
  questions as possible. ("Board the plane last" in the thumbnail, plus
  "the secret airlines keep" in the title, raises three questions.)

## Thumbnail tactics

- **One spotlight element** stands out from the page: an expressive
  **face**, a **short piece of text** (never an explanation), or a
  **familiar object**, ideally with something weird done to it.
  Simplicity makes the spotlight stronger.
- **An artistic look builds trust** (Lemmino); a deliberately crude look
  also stands out. A distinctive style in the niche works too.
- **Tell a story through subtle details:** posture, setting, lighting, and
  small text ("Day 2," "41 minutes in"). Ryan Trahan's thumbnail room is a
  staged set, bleaker than the real one, to sell the challenge.
- **Color sets the mood.** Warm colors suggest excitement, survival, and
  fun; cool colors suggest calm, intelligence, and creativity. Heavy
  saturation skews young. Arrows, circles, and borders control the order
  things are seen in.
- **Photograph and compose elements separately.** A screenshot of the
  video is one of the biggest mistakes.

- **Build it from scratch** to represent the video, not a literal frame
  from it (the pacifier, the impossible plane shot). The Manhunt exception:
  a staged "raw screenshot" works when authenticity and "no clickbait" is
  the selling point, but it's still deliberately set up.
- **Simplicity:** one element that says everything. Clutter hides every
  element.
- **Brightness contrast and color contrast** are maximized deliberately
  (the black puffer, the orange coat, a white background).
- **Recognition:** familiar things, faces, emojis, and brands grab
  attention first. A 5k-subscriber creator got about 500k views on a
  saturated topic largely thanks to an emoji.
- **Something alarming** (danger bias).
- **Look special:** a unique art style signals unique content. Leo's own
  channel used a unique thumbnail art style as its differentiator.
- **An accurate stylistic impression** of the experience, which matters
  most for documentaries on unfamiliar topics.
- **A facial expression reacting to something unseen:** shock and fear
  work best because they imply something extraordinary.
- **An unknown before or after:** a situation that makes you wonder what
  happened.

## Title and title-thumbnail tactics

- **Don't title literally.** Be dramatic, build curiosity, and offer a
  glimpse of the highlight rather than a summary (MrBeast titles the
  single coolest experiment). Leo's shortcut is to **title the video with
  its driving question.** Use a literal title only when anything else
  would confuse or clickbait.
- **Put the most important and interesting words first.** The topic word
  buried at the end is the most common wording mistake. Use short, common,
  clear words, and avoid titles long enough to get cut off.

- **Write in the new viewer's language,** not the creator's. "Minecraft
  Speedrunner vs 5 Hunters," not "Manhunt Ep. 24." It's the iPod lesson:
  "1,000 songs in your pocket" beat "6 GB MP3 player." It doesn't matter
  what you say, it matters what people hear.
- **Communicate the one or two most intriguing parts,** not the whole
  video. The video can open more topics once the viewer is hooked.
- **Clarity with uncertainty; never confusion.** Clarity is the
  foundation every other tactic depends on.
- **Curiosity triggers:** a hidden secret, a mystery or sense of wonder,
  a puzzle with an obviously missing piece (which must feel important), an
  unsolved problem, something surprising or counterintuitive, or a
  challenge to common beliefs.
- **Value and fear:** help reach goals or relieve pain, or warn that the
  viewer is making a mistake or is at risk.
- **Frame the ordinary as extraordinary** by searching for the best word
  ("I tested 1-star theme parks").
- **Promise a spectacle** and raise the expectations (the mannequins and
  "speed of sound" around a Beyblade).
- **Trust:** brand association (a known face, "military technique"),
  credentials, and proof.
- **"No freaking way":** a seemingly impossible premise (one versus five,
  cutting a table with plastic knives).
- **The Manhunt formula:** a very entertaining premise that seems
  impossible, communicated simply and with inclusive clarity.

## Idea checklist (before anything is shot)

- **Popular area of interest:** broad appeal beats depth on a niche
  feature ("the craziest seeds" versus "everything about one seed," which
  was a 1,000× difference). Find it by listing the top videos your
  audience watches (YouTube Studio → Analytics → Audience → channels and
  videos your audience watches, plus similar channels, each one's top 15
  videos) and looking for patterns in topics, formats, pain points, goals,
  wording, and tone.
- **Borrow outlier concepts from outside the niche,** but only concepts
  that match what the audience values (a helpful concept for a
  help-seeking audience, not an entertaining one).
- **Tests:**
  1. **A fresh look after a few days.** Most ideas don't survive.
  2. **Search whether it's been done.** If so, twist it or out-package it.
  3. **Is it the best option?** Aim for 5–10× outlier potential, and only
     take the top 10% of ideas.
  4. **Make the title and thumbnail first.** If you can't connect the
     audience's motivation to the video's value in them, the idea won't
     work.
- **An inherently entertaining premise:** a goal plus a second layer of
  conflict (antagonists, time limit, underdog). A good idea makes the
  video entertaining before recording starts.
- **Series and novelty:** a working format can be repeated many times if
  each episode brings fresh novelty. Dream bans any tactic once it's been
  used.
