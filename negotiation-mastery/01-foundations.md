# Module 1 — Foundations: The Physics of Every Negotiation

Before technique comes structure. Every negotiation — a hostage standoff, a merger, a salary discussion, a market stall in Delhi — obeys the same underlying mechanics. Learn these cold and you'll see the board while others only see their next move.

## 1. BATNA — your real source of power

**Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement** (Fisher & Ury). It's what you'll do if this negotiation fails.

- Your power in a negotiation is **exactly proportional to how good your BATNA is** — and how good the other side *believes* it is.
- **Always develop your BATNA before you negotiate.** Get a second job offer before the salary talk. Get a second vendor quote before the renewal call. Identify a second supplier before pressing your current one.
- **Estimate theirs.** Their BATNA defines how far you can push. A vendor with excess inventory and no other buyers has a weak BATNA; push. A star candidate with three offers has a strong one; don't bluff.
- Never reveal a weak BATNA. Never lie about having a strong one (if called, your credibility is gone for the rest of the relationship).

**Drill:** Before any negotiation, write one sentence: *"If this falls through, I will ______."* If you can't finish the sentence with something concrete, you are not ready to negotiate.

## 2. Reservation point and ZOPA

- **Reservation point:** the worst deal you'll accept before walking. Derive it from your BATNA, and set it **before** the conversation — in the room, adrenaline and sunk-cost will bend it.
- **ZOPA (Zone Of Possible Agreement):** the overlap between your reservation point and theirs. If there's no overlap, no amount of charm creates a deal — your job becomes changing the ZOPA (add issues, change scope, improve your BATNA) or walking.
- Most amateurs negotiate over a single number. Professionals **widen the ZOPA by adding variables**: timing, payment terms, scope, guarantees, exclusivity, future business. A deal impossible on price alone is often easy across five dimensions.

## 3. Anchoring — the strongest bias in bargaining

The first number on the table gravitationally pulls the final outcome toward it. This is one of the most replicated findings in negotiation research.

**When to anchor first (Harvard school):** when you know the market well. Anchor **ambitiously but justifiably** — the most extreme number you can defend with a straight face and a reason.

**When to let them anchor (Voss school):** when information is asymmetric against you — they may open better than you'd dare ask. Then **defuse their anchor** rather than counter-offer immediately:
- Label it: *"It seems like you have strong reasons for that number."*
- Ask a calibrated question: *"How did you arrive at that?"*
- Reframe to objective criteria: *"Help me understand how that compares to the market rate."*
- Never negotiate against their anchor by mentally adjusting from it. Return to **your** prepared target.

**Precision beats roundness:** an anchor of $37,850 outperforms $38,000 — precise numbers signal calculation and are moved less.

## 4. Creating value vs. claiming value

Every negotiation contains both games (Malhotra & Bazerman):

- **Claiming value** (distributive): dividing a fixed pie. Price haggling.
- **Creating value** (integrative): growing the pie by trading on **differences** — in priorities, timelines, risk tolerance, and forecasts. You care about price; they care about payment schedule. Trade.

The master move is **investigative negotiation**: before conceding anything, understand *why* they want what they want. The classic Fisher & Ury story: two sisters fight over an orange, split it in half — one wanted only the peel for baking, the other only the juice. Positions conflicted; interests didn't.

**Never make a concession without getting something.** Trade, don't give: *"If I can do X for you, could you do Y for me?"*

## 5. Leverage — and its three types (Shell)

1. **Positive leverage:** you have something they want.
2. **Negative leverage:** you can make them worse off (walk away, go public, sue). Powerful but corrosive — spend it rarely.
3. **Normative leverage:** their own stated standards, policies, and past behavior. *"Your website says customer satisfaction is your #1 value — help me square that with this outcome."* Cheap to use and hard to resist, because people hate visible inconsistency (see Cialdini's consistency principle, Module 4).

Leverage is **situational and perishable** — it shifts during the deal. The buyer has it before signing; the seller after. Time pressure destroys the leverage of whoever feels it more. **Never reveal your deadline; always probe for theirs.** (And know Voss's finding: deadlines are usually softer than claimed — most are self-imposed.)

## 6. The concession curve

How you concede signals as much as what you concede:

- **Decreasing increments.** $1,000 → $400 → $150 → $50 signals you're hitting bottom. Equal or increasing steps signal there's more.
- **Slowly.** Fast concessions devalue themselves and teach the other side that pressure works.
- **With a reason, every time.** An unexplained concession invites another demand; a justified one ("I can do this only because you're taking the annual plan") closes a door behind it.
- **Non-round final numbers** ($37,650, not $38K) read as the true limit of your math. This is the backbone of the Ackerman system (Module 6).

## 7. The fairness engine

Humans reject objectively good deals that feel unfair (the ultimatum-game result). "Fair" is the most powerful — and most abused — word in negotiation. Voss's rules:

- **Use it proactively:** *"I want you to feel treated fairly at all times. If at any point you feel I'm being unfair, stop me and we'll address it."* This disarms the F-bomb before it's dropped.
- **When it's used against you** (*"we just want what's fair"* as pressure), don't get defensive. Label and probe: *"It seems like you feel I'm being unfair. Let's go back to where that started — what would fair look like to you?"*

## 8. Emotions are data, not noise

The classic advice "keep emotions out of it" is wrong and impossible. Emotions are the primary channel of information (Voss, Diamond):

- **Theirs:** anger, hesitation, enthusiasm, and silence all telegraph interests and limits. Read them (Module 5), label them (Module 2).
- **Yours:** the goal is not suppression but **regulation**. Ury's "go to the balcony": when provoked, mentally step onto a balcony overlooking the negotiation and watch it as a spectator. Slow down. Lower your voice. The one who stays calm controls the frame.
- **Emotional payments** (Diamond): apologies, acknowledgment, respect, and small courtesies are currency. They cost nothing and buy real concessions, especially in relationship-first cultures (India, Japan — see Module 7).
