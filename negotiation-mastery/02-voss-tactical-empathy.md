# Module 2 — The Voss Method: Tactical Empathy

Chris Voss spent 24 years in the FBI, ending as its lead international kidnapping negotiator. His core discovery, distilled in *Never Split the Difference*: negotiation is not a rational contest — it's an emotional process, and **tactical empathy** (deliberately understanding and vocalizing the other side's feelings and worldview) is the fastest path to influence.

This module is the complete toolkit, technique by technique, with exact scripts. These work face-to-face, on the phone, and (adapted) in writing.

---

## 2.1 Voice: your first instrument

Three voices, deployed deliberately:

1. **The Late-Night FM DJ voice** — deep, slow, calm, downward-inflecting. Use when asserting or calming. It triggers a neurochemical calming response in the listener (and in you).
2. **The positive/playful voice** — your default, ~80% of the time. Smile while you talk, even on the phone; it's audible and contagious, and people in a positive frame think more collaboratively.
3. **The direct/assertive voice** — rarely. It signals dominance and invites pushback.

**Rule:** when the conversation heats up, slow down and drop your pitch. Speed reads as anxiety; calm reads as control.

## 2.2 Mirroring — the simplest, weirdest, most effective tool

Repeat the **last one to three words** (or the critical words) of what they just said, with an upward inflection. Then **shut up for at least four seconds**.

> Them: "We just can't go above fifty thousand because of the budget cycle."
> You: "The budget cycle?"
> Them: "Yeah, our fiscal year closes in March, and honestly if we could book it in Q1 instead..."

Mirrors feel awkward to do and invisible to receive. They make people **elaborate**, feel heard, and keep talking — every extra sentence they speak is free intelligence. Chain mirrors to peel an issue like an onion.

**Voss's late-night-DJ mirror combo for confrontation:** calm voice → "I'm sorry..." → mirror → 4+ seconds of silence → repeat.

## 2.3 Labeling — name the emotion, defuse or reinforce it

Verbally tag the emotion or dynamic you observe:

- *"It seems like you're under a lot of pressure to get this closed."*
- *"It sounds like there's something about this structure that doesn't sit right."*
- *"It looks like you've been burned by a vendor before."*

**Rules:**
- Start with *"It seems like / It sounds like / It looks like"* — never "What I'm hearing is..." (the word **I** makes it about you and invites correction as confrontation).
- After a label: **silence**. Let it work. They will either confirm and elaborate, or correct you — both are wins.
- Label **negatives to defuse them** (naming fear shrinks it — neuroscience shows labeling an emotion reduces amygdala activation) and **positives to reinforce them**.
- Wrong labels are cheap: they just say "no, it's more that..." — and hand you better information.

## 2.4 The Accusation Audit — say their worst thoughts first

Before delivering bad news or a big ask, **list every negative thing they could be thinking about you — out loud, up front, worse than they'd put it.**

> "You're probably going to think I'm being greedy. You might feel like I haven't been here long enough to ask for this, and that I'm putting you in a terrible spot with the rest of the team..."

Then make your ask. Because you've already named the accusations, they deflate; frequently the other side rushes to reassure you ("no, no, that's not unreasonable..."). Voss calls it taking the sting out before the slap. **Go worse than reality** — if you understate, you sound defensive; if you overstate, they correct you downward, in your favor.

## 2.5 "No" is the beginning, not the end

People feel trapped by "yes" (three kinds: counterfeit, confirmation, commitment — the first two are worthless) and **safe saying "no."** "No" gives the speaker control and protection; after it, they relax and actually listen.

**No-oriented questions** — flip your asks so "no" means yes:

| Instead of | Ask |
|---|---|
| "Do you have a few minutes to talk?" | **"Is now a bad time to talk?"** |
| "Do you agree with this approach?" | **"Would it be ridiculous to consider this approach?"** |
| "Can we move forward?" | **"Is there anything about this proposal that doesn't work for you?"** |
| "Do you want this project to succeed?" | **"Have you given up on solving this?"** |

**The email magic bullet** for being ignored — one line, nothing else:
> **"Have you given up on this project?"**
No one can resist correcting it, and it gets responses when weeks of polite follow-ups fail.

## 2.6 "That's right" — the breakthrough moment

Your target is not "yes." It's hearing **"that's right."**

Deliver a **summary** — a paraphrase of their facts *plus* a label of their feelings and worldview — so complete that they respond "that's right." At that moment they feel fully understood, and resistance collapses; it is the reliable turning point in Voss's hardest cases.

> "So you've built this team from nothing over six years, leadership keeps adding scope without adding headcount, and now a consultant who's been here three weeks is telling you to reorganize. It feels disrespectful to everything you've sacrificed."
> — "That's right."

**Warning:** *"You're right"* is the opposite — it's what people say to make you go away. If you hear "you're right," you've won nothing. Go back to labeling and summarizing.

## 2.7 Calibrated questions — the illusion of control

Open-ended questions starting with **"how"** or **"what"** (never "why" — it sounds accusatory in every language) that make the other side solve *your* problem while feeling in charge:

- **"How am I supposed to do that?"** — the gentle way to say "no" without saying no. Repeatable in varying forms; it forces them to consider your constraints.
- "What about this is important to you?"
- "How does this affect the rest of your team?"
- "What's the biggest challenge you face here?"
- "How can we solve this problem?"
- "What are we trying to accomplish here?"
- "How would we know we're on track?"

They buy you time, gather intelligence, avoid confrontation, and pull the other side into your problem. **Answer questions you don't like with a calibrated question of your own.**

**Self-control note:** when attacked, the calibrated question is your pause button. No more than three "How am I supposed to do that?" refusals in a row, though — after that it reads as stonewalling; change tools.

## 2.8 Guaranteeing execution — a "yes" without "how" is nothing

Deals die in implementation. Force the *how*:

- "How will we know we're on track?" / "How will we address it if we're off track?"
- **Beware "you're right" and vague agreement** — chase specifics.
- **Identify the hidden decision-makers:** "How does this affect everybody else?" "Who else needs to be on board?" The people *not* at the table kill more deals than the people at it.
- **The Rule of Three:** get the same commitment three times in one conversation, in three forms (agreement → label/summary of it → calibrated question about implementation). Liars struggle to fake it three times.
- **7-38-55:** Mehrabian's ratio (7% words, 38% tone, 55% body language, for emotional messages). When words and body/tone disagree, believe the body and tone — and label the gap: *"I heard you say yes, but it seemed like there was some hesitation."*
- **The Pinocchio effect:** liars use more words, more third-person pronouns, and more complex sentences. Note it, then triangulate with calibrated questions.

## 2.9 Bargaining hard: Ackerman + tactical tools

When it does come down to price, Voss uses the **Ackerman system** (full worked example in Module 6):

1. Set your target. Open at **65%** of it (buying) — extreme but justified anchor.
2. Concede in decreasing steps: **85% → 95% → 100%** of target.
3. Use **empathy and "How am I supposed to do that?"** between offers, so each raise is extracted from you, not offered.
4. Final number: **precise and non-round** (e.g., $34,826), plus a **non-monetary item** you probably don't even want — signaling you've scraped absolute bottom.

Also in the kit:
- **Range anchoring ("bolstering range"):** instead of one number, "at top firms, this role pays $130–140K" — the bottom of your range acts as the anchor and it's harder to attack.
- **Odd concessions:** trade in non-monetary currencies (title, start date, scope, publicity, introductions).
- **Strategic umbrage / flinch:** a visible, controlled reaction to an extreme offer resets expectations without a word.
- **"I'm sorry, that just doesn't work for me"** — the calm, complete sentence that replaces a counter-offer when their number is offensive. Silence after.

## 2.10 Black Swans — the unknown unknowns

Every negotiation contains **three to five pieces of hidden information that would change everything** if discovered (a deadline you don't know about, a boss driving the position, a prior trauma, a hidden constraint). Voss's rules for hunting them:

- Black Swans surface at the **margins**: before and after "the meeting," in small talk, in unguarded moments, from receptionists and juniors. Face time matters.
- **When something makes no sense, there's money to be made ("crazy" ≠ crazy):** irrational-seeming behavior signals a hidden constraint, a mistaken belief, or a hidden interest. Chase it with labels and calibrated questions instead of writing them off.
- **Leverage types to listen for:** positive (what they want), negative (what they fear), normative (their own rules and beliefs — worldview, religion, values; frame your ask inside *their* worldview and it becomes almost irresistible).
- Everything they tell you is a gift. **Talk less, listen more, and treat "we'd never..." statements as maps of their fears.**

## 2.11 The three negotiator types — calibrate your style to theirs

Voss's typology (diagnosis guide in Module 5):

| | **Analyst** | **Accommodator** | **Assertive** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prime value | Getting it *right* | The *relationship* | Getting it *done* |
| Time means | Preparation | Relationship-building | Money/efficiency |
| Silence means | Thinking (comfortable) | Anger/something wrong | Wants you to talk |
| Their risk | Paralysis, coldness | Overpromising, vagueness | Steamrolling, not listening |
| Negotiate with them by | Data, precision, no surprises, give them time | Warmth first, but pin down specifics ("how exactly...") | Let them talk first; mirrors + summaries until "that's right"; be direct |

**Golden rule:** don't treat them how *you'd* want to be treated — treat them how **they** need to be treated. And know your own type: your natural style is your default blind spot.

## The Voss conversation, end to end

1. Late-night DJ voice + positive tone. Small talk with intent (Black Swan hunting).
2. Accusation audit if the ask is hard.
3. Mirrors + labels until they're talking freely.
4. Summarize → **"that's right."**
5. Calibrated questions to shape the solution as *their* idea.
6. If bargaining: Ackerman. If refusing: "How am I supposed to do that?"
7. Rule of Three + "how" questions to lock execution.
8. Keep hunting Black Swans until the ink is dry.
