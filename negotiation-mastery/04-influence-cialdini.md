# Module 4 — The Science of Influence: Cialdini's Seven Principles

Robert Cialdini spent decades studying compliance professionals — salespeople, fundraisers, recruiters, con artists — and distilled *why people say yes* into principles so reliable they operate like psychological physics. In negotiation you need them twice over: **offense** (ethically deploying them) and **defense** (recognizing when they're aimed at you).

Ethics rule for everything below: use a principle only when it points at something **true**. Manufactured scarcity and fake authority work once, then destroy the trust that all future negotiations depend on.

---

## 1. Reciprocity

People feel a deep obligation to repay what they've received — favors, concessions, information, even attention.

**Offense:**
- **Give first, meaningfully, and unexpectedly.** Useful information, an introduction, flexibility on a minor term. The obligation it creates outlasts the gift's value.
- **Concession reciprocity (rejection-then-retreat):** open with a larger legitimate ask; your retreat to the real ask is experienced as a concession they must repay. (This is one engine inside Ackerman bargaining, Module 6.)
- Label your concessions so they register: "This is a real stretch for us, but..."
- When thanked, don't say "it was nothing." Say: *"Of course — I know you'd do the same for me."* (Plants future reciprocity.)

**Defense:** notice the gift-before-the-ask pattern (lavish dinners before contract talks). Accept graciously, and mentally **decouple**: "the meal was kind; the contract is the contract." Reciprocate hospitality with hospitality, not with margin.

## 2. Commitment & Consistency

People strain to act consistently with what they've already said and done — especially public, written, and voluntary commitments.

**Offense:**
- Get **small yeses early** ("Would you agree the goal is a long-term partnership?") — they constrain later behavior.
- Get commitments **in their words, in writing** (a recap email they confirm) — self-authored commitments bind hardest.
- Invoke *their* prior statements and policies as standards — this is normative leverage (Modules 1 & 3): "You said quality was the deciding factor..."

**Defense:** beware the low-ball (agree first, terms degrade later — car dealers' classic). Feel free to say: *"Knowing what I know now, I wouldn't have agreed then — so I'm not bound by it."* Consistency with a mistake is not a virtue.

## 3. Social Proof

Under uncertainty, people decide by watching what similar others do.

**Offense:**
- "Companies your size typically structure this as..." / "Most candidates at this level ask for..."
- The proof must come from **similar** others — peers, competitors, same industry and size. Testimonials from unlike parties don't move anyone.
- In cross-cultural deals, social proof from **their** in-group is worth 10× yours (see Module 7 — especially Japan and India).

**Defense:** "everyone accepts these terms" is a claim, not a fact. Ask: *"Which clients specifically? Could I speak with one?"* Watch uncertainty in yourself — that's when you're most susceptible.

## 4. Authority

People defer to credible expertise — credentials, track record, confident precision.

**Offense:**
- Establish expertise **before** the negotiation (bio, introduction by a third party, published work). Self-praise mid-negotiation backfires; arranged credibility doesn't.
- **Admit a weakness before your strongest point** ("The timeline is tight — *and* that's exactly why our senior team is assigned to it"). Trustworthy-then-strong beats strong-only.
- Precision signals authority: exact figures, named sources, fluent command of details (this powers the precise-anchor effect, Module 1).

**Defense:** ask two questions of any expert claim — *Is this person truly an expert here?* and *How truthful are they likely to be in this moment?* Titles and suits are symbols, not evidence.

## 5. Liking

People say yes to people they like. Liking is built from: **similarity, compliments, cooperation, and familiarity.**

**Offense:**
- Find genuine similarity early (background, interests, shared contacts, shared problems). Small talk isn't wasted time; it's the liking-building and Black-Swan-hunting phase (Module 2).
- Praise honestly and specifically. Behind-the-back compliments (relayed by others) are the most potent form.
- Frame everything structurally as **cooperation against a shared problem** — same side of the table, problem on the other side (Harvard Pillar 1).

**Defense:** the danger sign is liking the *negotiator* more than the *deal*. When you notice unusual warmth toward the person, deliberately re-evaluate the terms on paper, alone.

## 6. Scarcity

People want more of what they can have less of — and **loss framing beats gain framing** (prospect theory: losses loom roughly twice as large as gains).

**Offense:**
- State real limits: genuine deadlines, limited capacity, other interested parties — but only real ones.
- **Frame in losses:** "If we don't lock this quarter, the pilot slot goes to another region" outperforms "signing now gets you the pilot." Voss's version: show them what they stand to *lose* by no deal.
- Exclusive information ("this isn't public yet...") carries scarcity's full force.

**Defense:** scarcity pressure is the #1 manufactured tactic (fake deadlines, "another buyer this afternoon"). Antidote: **your prepared reservation point and BATNA** (Module 1). Panic is the tell — when you feel the clock, go to the balcony (Module 3) and ask: *would I want this deal at this price with no deadline?* Also remember Voss: most deadlines are self-imposed and negotiable.

## 7. Unity

Cialdini's later addition (*Pre-Suasion*): beyond liking — shared **identity**. Family, hometown, nationality, profession, "we"-groups. People say yes to *one of us*.

**Offense:**
- Surface genuine shared identity: alumni networks, industry tribes, regional roots, shared struggle ("we're both founders — we know what payroll week feels like").
- **Co-creation builds unity:** ask for their advice ("If you were me, how would you structure this?") — advisors become partners in your success. (Note how this rhymes with Voss's calibrated questions and Ury's golden bridge.)

**Defense:** invoked kinship ("we're like family here") right before an ask is a flag. Family doesn't need to say it.

---

## Pre-Suasion: the moment before the message

Cialdini's second big idea: **what you place in someone's attention immediately before your ask changes what they say yes to.** Attention makes things feel important and causal.

- Open with the frame you want them deciding inside: talk about *partnership* before proposing terms; discuss *quality failures they've suffered* before presenting your premium option; ask "do you consider yourself adventurous?" before proposing something novel.
- Environment counts: negotiate long-term partnerships over meals and in comfortable rooms, not across desks under fluorescent lights (see culture notes, Module 7 — in Japan and India the meal *is* the negotiation).
- **Defense:** notice what the other side puts in front of you first — the tour of the impressive office, the mention of their other bidders, the "just sign here" folder. The pre-frame is the tactic.

## The seven, mapped to a negotiation's arc

| Phase | Primary principles |
|---|---|
| Before the meeting | Authority (arranged credibility), Pre-suasion (frame) |
| Opening / rapport | Liking, Unity |
| Discovery | Reciprocity (give information first) |
| Proposal | Social proof, Authority (precision), Scarcity (loss framing) |
| Bargaining | Reciprocity (concession trading), Commitment (small yeses) |
| Closing | Consistency (their words, written), Scarcity (real deadlines) |
| After | Reciprocity ("you'd do the same for me"), Unity (shared success story) |
