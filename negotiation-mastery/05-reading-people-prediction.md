# Module 5 — Reading People & Predicting Behavior

The "predictor" module. Great negotiators aren't mind readers — they're **hypothesis machines**: they baseline, observe deviations, type their counterpart, model incentives, and update constantly. This module combines Joe Navarro's FBI-grade nonverbal work, Voss's typology and verbal tells, and structural prediction (incentives + constraints beat intuition).

A humility note up front: no single cue "means" lying. Modern deception research is clear that **there is no Pinocchio's nose** — what you can reliably detect is *stress, comfort, and change from baseline*, which tell you **where to dig with questions**.

---

## 5.1 Baselining: the foundation of all reading

You cannot interpret behavior without knowing what's normal *for this person, in this context*.

- Spend the first minutes on neutral topics (travel, weather, sports, background) and record their **baseline**: talking speed, eye contact pattern, posture, gesture level, laughter, response latency.
- Everything after is read as **deviation from baseline**, not against a universal codebook. A fidgeter fidgeting means nothing; a statue fidgeting means everything.
- Re-baseline across cultures: normal eye contact in New York is aggressive in Tokyo; normal directness in Frankfurt is rudeness in Delhi (Module 7). **Never read culture as personality.**

## 5.2 The body, in order of honesty (Navarro)

Navarro's key insight: the **limbic system leaks** — freeze/flight/fight responses show in the body before and despite the words. Reliability, from most honest to least:

1. **Feet and legs** — the most honest part of the body. Feet pointed at the door = they want out. A foot that starts bouncing or freezes on a topic = the topic matters. Toes lifting ("happy feet") = genuine positive excitement.
2. **Torso** — leaning away, angling, shielding with objects (laptop, folder) = discomfort/distancing. Ventral fronting (full torso facing you, open) = comfort and engagement.
3. **Arms and hands** — sudden withdrawal of hands, gripping the chair, interlaced tightening. **Pacifying behaviors** = self-soothing under stress: neck touching (highest-value tell), face rubbing, playing with jewelry/watch, ventilating a collar, leg-rubbing under the table. A cluster of pacifiers right after a topic = that topic hit a nerve.
4. **Face** — the *least* reliable, because it's the most consciously controlled. Still useful: lip compression (negative emotion held in), jaw shift, eye-blocking (touching/covering eyes at unwelcome information), a real (Duchenne, eye-crinkling) vs. social smile.

**Method: topic → cluster → probe.** One cue is noise. A **cluster of deviations timed to a specific topic** is signal. When you see it, don't announce it — return to that topic later, gently, with a label or a calibrated question: *"It seemed like the delivery-date question landed differently — what should I know about that?"*

## 5.3 Verbal and paraverbal tells

- **7-38-55 (Mehrabian):** in emotionally loaded messages, words carry ~7% of the message; tone ~38%; body ~55%. When channels disagree, **believe tone and body — and label the gap** (Module 2).
- **Response latency shifts:** sudden slow, careful answers from a fast talker (or the reverse) mark rehearsal or discomfort.
- **The Pinocchio effect (Voss):** deceptive statements tend to run longer, with more third-person pronouns and more complex sentence structure — over-engineering credibility.
- **Distancing language:** "that woman," "the vehicle," passive voice ("mistakes were made") pushes the speaker away from the content.
- **Unprompted qualifiers:** "to be honest," "frankly," "trust me" clusters around exactly the material that needs the propping.
- **Non-answers:** answering a different question than asked, repeating your question back verbatim (stalling), or answering with a question. Note it, re-ask once, plainly.
- **The Rule of Three (Module 2)** is your lie-triangulator: get the commitment three times in three forms. Inconsistency across the three tellings is the tell.

## 5.4 Typing your counterpart: Voss's three types

Diagnose within the first ten minutes and calibrate (full treatment table in Module 2 §2.11):

| Signal | Analyst | Accommodator | Assertive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pace | Slow, deliberate | Chatty, warm | Fast, blunt |
| Small talk | Minimal, tolerated | Loves it | Impatient with it |
| Data | Wants everything in advance | Prefers stories | Wants the headline |
| Silence | Comfortable — they're thinking | Distressing — they fill it | A vacuum they expect *you* to fill |
| Prediction | Won't decide in the room; will re-verify everything | Will overpromise; execution is the risk | Will anchor aggressively; respects pushback more than deference |

**Predictive payoffs:** expect the Analyst to go silent after your proposal (don't chase it — schedule the follow-up); expect the Accommodator's "yes" to need Rule-of-Three verification; expect the Assertive's first "final offer" not to be final.

## 5.5 Structural prediction: incentives beat intuition

The most reliable predictions come not from body language but from **modeling the counterpart's situation**. Before the meeting, answer in writing:

1. **BATNA:** What happens to them if there's no deal? How bad is it, really?
2. **Clock:** What deadlines do *they* face (fiscal year end, quota, boss's review, expiring inventory, funding round)? People behave predictably near their own deadlines — sellers concede at quarter-end, buyers rush before budget expiry.
3. **Principal–agent gap:** Is the person in the room rewarded for the same thing their organization needs? A procurement officer bonused on % savings will fight for optics of discount — give them a "20% off" story on an inflated list price and everyone wins. An agent paid on commission wants *close speed*, not top price.
4. **Audience:** Whom must they justify this deal to? Their victory speech (Ury's golden bridge, Module 3) is a design requirement. Predict their must-have headline and build it in.
5. **Precedent-fear:** Large organizations resist deals that create precedents more than deals that cost money. If you hit precedent-resistance, restructure (one-time exception, different label, side letter) rather than push size.
6. **Consistency trap (Cialdini):** What have they publicly said and done before? They will strain to remain consistent with it — that's a rail their behavior runs on.
7. **Loss aversion:** They will fight ~2× harder to avoid a visible loss than to gain the same amount. Predict irrational-looking resistance wherever your proposal makes someone's current holding, budget, headcount, or status *shrink*.

**When behavior seems crazy, your model is missing a variable** (Voss's "crazy = clue"). Hidden constraints, hidden interests, bad information. Hunt the Black Swan before writing them off.

## 5.6 The prediction loop (run it every negotiation)

```
1. PRE-BRIEF   Write 3 predictions: their opening position, their real
               reservation point, their #1 hidden interest. Assign confidence.
2. BASELINE    First 5-10 minutes: neutral topics, record their normal.
3. TYPE        Analyst / Accommodator / Assertive → adjust your style.
4. TEST        Labels and calibrated questions as probes; track deviations
               (topic → cluster → probe).
5. UPDATE      Revise your 3 predictions mid-meeting. What surprised you
               is where the Black Swan lives.
6. DEBRIEF     After: score your predictions. What did you miss and why?
```

The debrief is the step almost no one does, and it's the one that builds the skill. Ten scored debriefs will improve your read more than a hundred unexamined negotiations.

## 5.7 Reading the room (multi-party)

- **Watch who watches whom.** When a hard question lands, eyes flick to the real decision-maker — org charts lie, glances don't.
- The most senior person often talks least (especially in Japan and Germany — Module 7). Note whom the talkers check for approval.
- Track **who goes quiet** on which topic — silence is topical data.
- Seating is data: allies cluster; the skeptic often sits farthest away or perpendicular.

## 5.8 Defense: managing your own tells

Everything above is being read off *you* by anyone competent.

- **Decide your reveal policy in preparation, not in the moment.** Know what you'll share freely (builds reciprocity), what you'll trade, and what you'll protect.
- Under pressure: slow your speech, drop your pitch (late-night DJ), keep your hands visible and still, and **beware your own pacifiers** — know what yours are (most people don't).
- The strongest tell-suppressor is real: **a strong BATNA makes calm authentic.** You can't fake not needing the deal nearly as well as you can actually not need it.
