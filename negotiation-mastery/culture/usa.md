# Country Playbook — USA

**Archetype:** low-context, task-based trust, fast clock, deal-focused, top-down decisions, comfortable with open bargaining. The US is the home turf of most of this guide's source material (Voss, Harvard, Cialdini), so the full toolkit applies natively — but Americans have specific, exploitable patterns too.

## Culture Map position
- **Communicating:** the world's most low-context major culture. Say it explicitly, recap it in writing, "tell them what you told them." Subtlety will simply be missed — not decoded.
- **Trusting:** task-based. Trust comes from demonstrated competence and delivered promises, fast. Relationship warmth is genuine but instant and thin ("friendly ≠ friends") — don't mistake first-name banter and personal questions for a bond that will protect you in bargaining.
- **Deciding:** top-down and fast. The person in the room often *can* decide (unlike Japan/Germany). Find the decision-maker and you can close in one meeting.
- **Evaluating:** negative feedback is wrapped in positives. Read "this is great, a few thoughts..." as potentially serious objections.
- **Scheduling:** linear and impatient. Time is money is not a metaphor here; speed itself is a value.

## How Americans negotiate
- **They open with optimism and ambition.** Expect confident, sometimes aggressive anchors, salesmanship, and superlatives ("best-in-class"). Discount the adjectives; price the substance.
- **They bargain openly and expect you to.** Haggling over terms is normal, not offensive. "What'll it take to get this done?" is a real question.
- **Risk-tolerant and experiment-friendly:** pilots, trials, "let's just try it and fix it later" are attractive propositions.
- **The contract is the deal.** Exhaustive, lawyered, and binding. Expect legal review as standard, not distrust. Verbal agreement in the room + redlines for weeks is normal.
- **Litigation-awareness shapes behavior** — indemnities, liability caps, and IP clauses will get fought over hardest.
- **Money is the master metric.** ROI, payback period, and quarterly impact are the persuasion language. Applications-first (Meyer): lead with results and case studies, not theory. Give the bottom line up front, then support it.

## Your playbook
1. **Speed is credibility.** Respond fast, decide fast, and show momentum. Slowness reads as weakness or disinterest, not deliberation.
2. **Anchor confidently with comps.** Americans respect an aggressive, well-supported open. Precise numbers + named benchmarks (Module 1).
3. **Sell the upside AND quantify it.** US persuasion runs on possibility framed in numbers: "this saves $340K in year one."
4. **Use their clock.** Quarter-end and fiscal-year-end are real, powerful deadlines for US salespeople and executives (Module 5 §5.5). Concessions bloom in the last week of the quarter.
5. **Negotiate everything at once, not sequentially.** Package deals ("if we move on price, term, and support together...") suit the US preference for closing.
6. **Get it in writing immediately.** Same-day recap email; the summarizer wins (Module 6). They will do the same to you — check their recaps line by line.
7. **Expect and use directness.** "That doesn't work for us" is acceptable and clear. The full Voss kit — accusation audits, "How am I supposed to do that?", no-oriented questions — runs at full power here.
8. **Watch the false-consensus trap:** the enthusiastic VP saying "love it, let's do it" may still be overruled by procurement, legal, or the CFO. Rule of Three + "who else needs to be on board?" (Module 2 §2.8).

## Reading them / prediction notes
- Enthusiasm is the default register — calibrate down. "Awesome" = fine. "I love it" = interested. Real commitment = calendar invites, legal engaged, numbers in writing.
- The dangerous silence: Americans rarely go silent; when one does, something is genuinely wrong. Label it immediately.
- "We'll think about it and circle back" with no scheduled next step usually = a soft no. Force specificity: "What would need to be true for this to be a yes?"
- Job mobility means your counterpart may be gone in a year — **get institutional commitments, not personal ones**, into the contract.

## Rapport accelerants and landmines
- **Accelerants:** competence displayed early, humor, sports/college small talk (brief!), optimism, "getting to the point," win-win framing.
- **Landmines:** condescension about American culture; long silences; refusing to give a number ("it depends" without follow-through reads as evasive); slow email response; over-formality (using "Dr./Mr." after they've said "call me Mike").
- Meals are friendly but optional — deals close in conference rooms and over video calls. Don't be offended by a working lunch of sandwiches.

## Micro-phrasebook
- Opening a hard ask: *"I'm going to be straight with you, because I think you'll appreciate that..."*
- Pushing back: *"Help me understand how that math works, because from where I sit..."*
- Extracting the real limit: *"If price weren't the issue, would we have a deal?"* (isolates the objection)
- Closing: *"What has to happen on your side for us to sign this week?"*
