# Module 3 — The Harvard Method: Principled Negotiation

*Getting to Yes* (Fisher, Ury & Patton, Harvard Negotiation Project) is the most influential negotiation framework ever published. Where Voss gives you the conversational micro-tools, Harvard gives you the strategic architecture. Masters use both: Harvard to design the deal, Voss to conduct the conversation.

## The core diagnosis: positional bargaining fails

Haggling over positions ("$50K!" — "$40K!" — "$45K?") is inefficient, damages relationships, and produces worse outcomes — because egos fuse to positions, and every concession feels like losing face. Both "hard" and "soft" positional styles lose: hard wins battles and destroys relationships; soft preserves relationships and gets eaten alive.

The alternative is **principled negotiation** — four pillars:

## Pillar 1: Separate the people from the problem

Every negotiation runs on two tracks: the substance and the relationship. Entangling them is the root of most failures.

- **Be soft on the people, hard on the problem.** Attack the issue ferociously while treating the human with respect. These are not in tension — they reinforce each other.
- Deal with the three people-problems directly:
  - **Perception:** put yourself in their shoes; discuss each side's perceptions openly; never blame.
  - **Emotion:** recognize and legitimize emotions (yours and theirs); allow venting without reacting; use symbolic gestures (apology, acknowledgment). *(This is where Voss's labels plug in.)*
  - **Communication:** listen actively, speak to be understood, speak about yourself not about them ("I feel let down" vs. "you broke your word").
- **Face-saving is not vanity — it's a requirement.** People will reject a good deal that makes them look bad. Build them a story they can tell their boss, spouse, or team. (In Japan, India, and much of the world, this is *the* central mechanic — see Module 7.)

## Pillar 2: Focus on interests, not positions

A **position** is what they say they want. An **interest** is *why* they want it. Positions are usually incompatible; interests usually aren't.

- Behind every position sit multiple interests — and behind those, **basic human needs: security, economic wellbeing, belonging, recognition, control over one's life.** Deals that ignore these die even when the numbers work.
- Excavate interests with: *"Why do you want that?" "Why not this alternative?" "What would that solve for you?"* (Voss translation: "What makes that important to you?")
- **State your interests vividly and specifically** — people can't accommodate what they don't understand. Be hard on your interests while staying open on the means.
- Make a written list of both sides' interests, ranked. This list is the raw material for Pillar 3.

## Pillar 3: Invent options for mutual gain

Most negotiations leave enormous value on the table because of four blocks: premature judgment, searching for "the single answer," assuming a fixed pie, and "their problem is their problem."

- **Separate inventing from deciding.** Brainstorm options — alone or even jointly with the other side — with criticism explicitly suspended. Decide later.
- **Trade on differences.** Differences in priorities, risk tolerance, time horizon, and forecasts are the engine of value:
  - You want price, they want cash-flow timing → seller financing.
  - You believe the product will sell big, they doubt it → lower fixed fee + royalty (contingent contract).
  - They fear risk, you can absorb it → you take the risk, charge for it.
- **Make their decision easy.** Draft the "yesable proposition" — the single sentence they can say yes to — and the story they'll tell their constituents.

## Pillar 4: Insist on objective criteria

When interests directly conflict, don't settle by willpower — settle by **standards independent of will**: market value, precedent, replacement cost, professional norms, law, reciprocity, expert opinion.

- **Frame every issue as a joint search for the right standard:** "How are you determining that price?" "What would make this fair to both of us?"
- Reason, and be open to reason — but **never yield to pressure**, only to principle. "I'd be happy to reconsider if you can show me why that standard is wrong."
- Multiple standards usually exist (asking price vs. appraised value vs. comparable sales); negotiate over *which standard applies*, which is far less ego-fused than negotiating raw numbers.
- This is normative leverage (Module 1): their own policies, statements, and precedents are the strongest standards of all.

## BATNA revisited: the answer to "what if they're more powerful?"

Covered in Module 1; the Harvard additions:

- **Develop, don't just know, your BATNA:** invent alternatives, improve the best ones into real options, then pick your best and measure every proposal against it.
- **Consider their BATNA too** — if it's too good, your job is to worsen it (legitimately) or improve what you offer relative to it.
- The trap to avoid: negotiating to protect your **bottom line** (rigid, arbitrary) instead of your **BATNA** (real, comparative).

## Ury's *Getting Past No*: the breakthrough strategy for hard bargainers

When the other side stonewalls, attacks, or tricks, the natural reactions — striking back, giving in, breaking off — all lose. Ury's five-step system:

1. **Go to the balcony.** Don't react. Name the tactic to yourself, pause, say nothing, or "rewind the tape" ("let me make sure I understand where we are"). Buy time to think. *You cannot be provoked into anything if you refuse to react on their clock.*
2. **Step to their side.** Do the opposite of arguing: listen, acknowledge their point, their feelings, their competence and status. Agree wherever you honestly can ("yes... and" never "but"). Disarm before you engage.
3. **Reframe.** Don't reject their position — treat it as one option and redirect: ask problem-solving questions ("why is that important?", "what if we...?", "what's wrong with this alternative?"), reinterpret attacks as attacks on the problem, expose tactics by naming them gently ("I'll assume that deadline is flexible while we work this out").
4. **Build them a golden bridge.** Make saying yes easy: involve them so the solution is *their* idea, satisfy the unmet interests behind their resistance (often face, recognition, or fear), help them write the victory speech they'll give to their side, go slow to go fast.
5. **Use power to educate, not escalate.** If they still refuse, don't threaten — **warn** (consequences that happen *to* them by default, not *by* you), demonstrate your BATNA without humiliating them, and keep the golden bridge open the entire time. The goal is mutual satisfaction, not victory: "the best general is the one who never fights."

## Ury's *Power of a Positive No*: refusing without destroying

When you must say no — to a client, a boss, a family member — deliver it as **"Yes! → No. → Yes?"**:

1. **Yes!** — affirm the deeper interest or value you're protecting ("My weekends belong to my kids...").
2. **No.** — a calm, matter-of-fact boundary ("...so I don't take weekend calls...").
3. **Yes?** — a positive alternative ("...but I can be your first call Monday morning. Would that work?").

This is how you defend your reservation point (Module 1) without detonating the relationship.

## Harvard × Voss: one integrated system

| Situation | Harvard gives you | Voss gives you |
|---|---|---|
| Designing the deal | Interests map, options, standards | — |
| Opening the conversation | — | Tone, mirror, label, accusation audit |
| They state a hard position | "Why? What's the interest behind it?" | *"It seems like that number is important to you — how did you get to it?"* |
